Journal of Edmund Lott, Jr.
September 3–October 21, 1888

Monday, September 3, 1888
Clear and warm.
Ten years today since Grandfather died. I went to his grave after breakfast and found the grass much grown about the stones. Told William to have it cut before the end of the week.
The cotton in the south field is coming along better than I expected. William believes we may begin gathering there within another fortnight. There is still trouble with the north fence, where the cattle have been getting through.
Spent the evening examining Grandfather's notes. October 11 approaches, and I have not yet made all the arrangements I intended.
Victoria asked whether I meant to sit up again. I told her I had some accounts to finish.

Tuesday, September 4, 1888
Cloudy until noon.
William had the men repairing the fence by the lower pasture. One of the mules has a swelling above the left foreleg. We shall have to rest him for several days.
Received word that the electrical apparatus ordered through Bell may arrive before the end of the month. I have asked Bellamy to prepare a list of what we already possess, so that nothing need be purchased twice.
Victoria wishes to visit her mother in Fort Worth next month. I see no objection, provided the harvest is sufficiently advanced.
Read until eleven. There are several passages in Grandfather's papers which I understand better now than I did ten years ago.

Wednesday, September 5, 1888
Hot.
Walked the eastern boundary with William. The creek has cut farther into the bank since the last heavy rain. William says a portion of the old wagon road is scarcely fit for a loaded cart.
Bell's claim concerning the road continues to trouble him. I told him there is little to be gained by arguing until the measurements are settled.
Two hands were absent this morning. William says one has gone to see a sick relation and the other has not given any explanation.
Mrs. Hester served beans and salt pork for supper. Victoria complained of the heat in the upstairs rooms.
I spent an hour arranging the compass and checking its case.

Thursday, September 6, 1888
Fair.
William and I reviewed the accounts for the coming harvest. There will be expense in hiring another wagon if the picking proceeds as he expects. The chain on our own wagon needs replacing.
Rusk came by after dinner and remained for some time. William has asked him more than once to take charge of the field hands, but he declined again today. William was disappointed. He believes Rusk could keep better order than the men presently employed.
I have no particular opinion on the matter. Rusk is useful when there is work requiring a strong back and a steady hand.
One of the calves got into Mrs. Hester's beans. She was much displeased.
No word from Bell concerning the remaining apparatus.

Friday, September 7, 1888
Rain in the morning.
The fields were too wet for much work. William sent two men to clear the drainage ditch near the barn.
Bellamy came after breakfast. We examined the compass and the galvanometer and found the latter in need of attention at one connection. He believes he can repair it without sending for another.
I have been comparing the figures from the old survey with Grandfather's measurements. There is a difference which I cannot yet account for. The creek may have changed its course.
Victoria asked why I have taken such an interest in that particular piece of ground. I told her it was a matter of the property lines, which is true enough.

Saturday, September 8, 1888
Fine weather.
The men returned to the fields. William says the cotton will be ready sooner than he had thought.
Rode into town for writing paper, lamp oil, and several small articles for the house. Paid the smith for work done on the wagon.
I saw Bell at his store and asked whether the shipment had been heard from. He said the goods were expected by way of New Orleans and that he would send word when they arrived. He has dealt fairly with me in these purchases.
Returned late in the afternoon. Victoria had visitors, and I remained in the study until supper.

Sunday, September 9, 1888
Attended services with Victoria.
She spoke again of our having children. I told her there would be time enough after the harvest and that I wished first to put certain affairs in order. She seemed dissatisfied with the answer.
I have delayed the matter longer than I ought. Grandfather was not a young man when he entrusted his papers to me, and I remember his insistence that the family must continue.
After dinner I walked as far as the upper pasture. The weather has been favorable, though the nights are beginning to cool.
Read a little before bed.

Monday, September 10, 1888
Clear. Temperature at eight in the morning, 76 degrees; at two in the afternoon, 88. Barometer 29.91.
Went with Bellamy to Mercy Crossing and examined the place selected for the instrument. The stone foundation measures approximately three feet square. We shall have to level the upper surface and remove the roots at its eastern side.
Bellamy had been drinking last evening and was slow at his work. I told him I would not tolerate such carelessness when the apparatus arrives.
Grandfather's papers refer more than once to the circumstances of his father's death in Massachusetts. The accounts are incomplete. I have sometimes wondered whether the old stories of the Necronomicon have any foundation, though I know of no proof that Benjamin possessed such a volume.
Mrs. Hester served roast beef. Very good.

Tuesday, September 11, 1888
Cloudy until noon.
William began preparing the wagons for cotton. One axle requires grease, and a wheel on the north wagon has become loose.
I had Bellamy bring the compass to the crossing. The needle settled ordinarily, though the reading differed slightly from the figure taken last week.
We marked two points on the bank and measured between them. I want a fixed distance by which to judge any movement of the ground.
Victoria has begun making arrangements for her journey. She says she may remain with her mother a fortnight.
There was thunder after dark, but little rain.

Wednesday, September 12, 1888
Warm and close.
The first cotton was gathered from the higher ground. William says the bolls are opening unevenly and that we must return to several rows later.
Spent the morning with the accounts. There is more owing to the merchant than I had supposed.
Bellamy brought a length of wire and several fittings which he obtained in town. We tested the connections in the study. The current was insufficient for the purpose intended, and we shall need the larger battery.
I read Grandfather's notes again after supper. He was exact in some matters and curiously silent in others.

Thursday, September 13, 1888
Rain during the night.
The lower road was muddy. William took the lighter wagon to town and returned with nails, a sack of flour, and two parcels for Victoria.
I remained at the house and copied several passages from Grandfather's notebook. Some of the measurements appear to have been taken at intervals of many years.
Bellamy called in the afternoon. We agreed upon a place for the battery and a shelter for the smaller instruments.
Mrs. Hester says the roof above the kitchen is leaking again. I have promised to have it examined before winter.

Friday, September 14, 1888
Fair and cooler.
The men worked in the south field throughout the day. William is pleased with the amount gathered.
Rode to the crossing after dinner. The bank is firm where we placed the marks, though there is loose earth nearer the old road.
I saw Bell's wagon at a distance. There is nothing remarkable in this, his mill being near the creek. William nevertheless complains that Bell treats the road as though it belongs to him alone.
I told William the matter should be left to a surveyor.
Victoria has asked me to accompany her to church on Sunday. I agreed.

Saturday, September 15, 1888
Clear.
One of the field hands cut his foot upon a broken bottle. William sent him home and says he will lose several days' work.
Rusk stopped in the afternoon and remained for supper. He and William spoke of the harvest and the condition of the wagons. William complained that the men were not working quickly enough.
Rusk said there had been trouble concerning the road by Cypress Bend, though he offered few particulars. I told them that the matter ought to be settled by measurement rather than argument.
Victoria was in good spirits and played a little at the piano after supper.
I retired early but did not sleep until late.

Sunday, September 16, 1888
Went to church in Nacogdoches.
Dined afterward with Uncle Peter. He asked about the harvest and whether William intended to purchase more land. I told him we have enough to occupy us at present.
Peter speaks of Grandfather as though he were chiefly a man of property. He never understood the extent of Grandfather's learning. Father was much the same, though a capable man in practical affairs.
I have often thought it strange that our family should have come so far from Massachusetts and yet retained so little interest in its earlier history.
Victoria enjoyed the visit. We returned before sunset.

Monday, September 17, 1888
Hot.
William put additional men into the cotton. There was some complaint about the division of the rows, which he settled.
I examined the foundation at the crossing and found the upper stones uneven. Bellamy and I worked there until noon. We shall need a little mortar.
A letter came from Victoria's mother. She is not well, though Victoria believes the complaint is not serious. I encouraged her to make the visit.
The compass was steady throughout the afternoon.
Read until midnight.

Tuesday, September 18, 1888
Clear.
Paid two men for repairing the fence by the eastern pasture. One of the posts had rotted below the ground.
William says we must purchase another mule before spring. I told him to wait until the cotton is sold.
Bellamy brought the mortar to the crossing. We set the upper stones and left them to harden. The work was satisfactory.
I have begun a separate book for the observations. It will be easier to compare the figures if they are kept apart from the ordinary accounts.
Victoria says I am becoming a stranger in my own house.

Wednesday, September 19, 1888
Rain before breakfast.
Little work was done in the fields. Mrs. Hester had the men carry firewood beneath the shed, where it may remain dry.
I spent most of the day examining the old papers. Grandfather recorded several readings near the crossing but did not explain why he considered them important.
There is also an account of a survey made in 1856. The compass was said to have behaved irregularly on one evening. I should like to know what instrument was used and whether the surveyor made any correction for variation.
Bellamy called after dinner. We discussed the battery and the length of wire required.

Thursday, September 20, 1888
Fair.
The men returned to picking. William says we are now behind where we ought to be.
Went to town for a parcel Victoria had ordered. Met Bell and asked again about the shipment. He expects word shortly and offered to have the heavier articles carried as far as our road when they arrive.
He mentioned the disagreement over the crossing. I told him that I had no wish to interfere with his property and that the question of the road would be properly examined.
He was civil, though not satisfied.

Friday, September 21, 1888
Warm.
The mule's leg is improved, but William does not wish to put him back to work yet.
Bellamy and I made another examination of the foundation. It is now sufficiently firm for the apparatus.
I took several compass readings. There was no unusual movement.
Returned to the house before dinner. Victoria had been arranging her clothes for the journey and asked whether I would miss her. I said of course I would.
I have been thinking of the approaching anniversary. Grandfather died on September 3, but October 11 was the date he marked most carefully in his papers.

Saturday, September 22, 1888
Clear.
Cotton picking continued until late afternoon. The north wagon became stuck near the lower field. Rusk happened to be passing and helped William get it free. He would accept no payment.
William says he would trust Rusk with any work on the property. He still regrets that Rusk will not take the position he offered.
One mule has gone lame again. I fear we shall have to replace him.
Mrs. Hester requires a new kettle. The present one has cracked near the handle.
I spent the evening arranging my notes. The next several weeks will require more attention than I have lately been able to give them.

Sunday, September 23, 1888
Cool in the morning.
Victoria and I remained at home. She read in the parlor while I went over the accounts.
William came after dinner to discuss the price of cotton. He believes we should hold part of the crop rather than sell everything at once.
Walked to Grandfather's grave in the afternoon. The grass has been cut, and the place is in good order.
I remember standing there with him when I was a boy, while he pointed out the old road and the bend of the creek. He told me that a man ought to know the ground upon which his family stood.
I did not then understand how much he meant by it.

Monday, September 24, 1888
Clear and dry.
The harvest proceeds well. William has hired another wagon for the week.
Bellamy repaired a loose connection in the galvanometer. We tested it several times and obtained satisfactory results.
I wrote to inquire whether the remaining equipment had reached the depot.
Victoria has arranged to depart early next month. I shall be glad when the journey is settled, for she has spoken of little else.
There is much expense in the household at present. I must be more attentive to the accounts.

Tuesday, September 25, 1888
Hot.
Rode along the eastern line and found a portion of the bank washed away. The old marks are not so dependable as I should wish.
William says the creek has moved within his own recollection. This may account for the disagreement between the surveys.
At the crossing I measured the distance from the foundation to the marked tree. Bellamy recorded the figure.
Returned late and found Victoria displeased that I had missed dinner. I apologized.
Spent an hour before bed reading the family papers. Grandfather's hand was very steady even in his last years.

Wednesday, September 26, 1888
Cloudy.
The cotton in the south field is nearly finished. William expects to begin the western rows tomorrow.
There was trouble over a broken harness. I authorized the purchase of a replacement.
Bellamy and I checked the compass again. The needle returned promptly to its former position.
I have ordered that the battery be kept in a dry room until we are ready to move it to the crossing.
Mrs. Hester prepared a pie from the last of the summer fruit. Victoria says we shall have fewer such pleasures when the weather turns.

Thursday, September 27, 1888
Clear.
Went to Mercy Crossing with Bellamy to repeat the distance measurements.
From the marked tree to the eastern point we obtained 126 feet 4 inches, then 126 feet 2 inches, and finally 126 feet 3 inches. Mean, 126 feet 3 inches. The small differences may be attributed to the chain and the uneven ground.
The foundation has been leveled and is ready.
I examined several passages in the Family Book after supper. There are references to Benjamin's affairs in Massachusetts, though not enough to settle the old accusations. Grandfather never told me that Benjamin owned the Necronomicon, and I have found no record proving it.
I sometimes think the stories have grown larger with each telling.

Friday, September 28, 1888
Rain during the night.
The road was too soft for the heavier wagon. William sent the men to repair a section of fence instead.
A letter arrived concerning the apparatus. The shipment has been delayed but should reach us within a few days.
I have written down the observations I intend to make on October 11. The hours must be kept precisely, and Bellamy must understand that no reading is to be omitted merely because it appears mistaken.
Victoria has begun to pack.
I was awake late, though there was no particular cause.

Saturday, September 29, 1888
Fair.
Went to town with William. Paid the merchant and arranged for the repair of the wagon wheel.
There was much talk of the coming election. I took little part in it.
Bell had not yet received the final parcel, though he believes it is on the way. He said he would send word directly.
William spoke again of the road. I told him I had already discussed the matter with Bell and did not wish it made into a quarrel.
Returned before dark. Mrs. Hester had prepared supper for several visitors, who remained until nine.

Sunday, September 30, 1888
Clear and pleasant.
Attended church with Victoria. Afterward we called upon acquaintances and returned home in the afternoon.
She asked whether I had finished the work which has occupied me so much lately. I told her the principal observations were still to come.
We spoke again of children. I said that when she returned from Fort Worth we should give the matter our attention. She was pleased, though she asked why I had changed my mind.
I told her I had been thinking more seriously about the future.
Spent the evening quietly.

Monday, October 1, 1888
Clear.
The instrument arrived today, together with the galvanometer fittings, battery connections, and other articles ordered through Bell.
William, Bellamy, Rusk, and four of the field hands assisted in taking the heavier pieces to the crossing. The road was troublesome in two places, and Rusk went ahead to clear a fallen limb.
He remarked that Bell continued to object to our use of the road. I told him the family had used that way for many years and that a surveyor ought to settle the question.
The instrument was placed upon the stone base, though the electrical connections remain to be completed.
William asked why I required so much equipment for a survey. I told him that I intended to make more exact measurements than had previously been taken.
Victoria asked the same question at supper. I told her that when she returned we should have time to consider the child we had spoken of.
I have accomplished more in these last weeks than Father ever thought possible in this matter. Grandfather would have understood the importance of it.

Tuesday, October 2, 1888
Warm.
Bellamy and I spent the morning arranging the apparatus. The battery was carried to the crossing in its case and placed beneath the shelter.
We found that one of the connections required a longer wire. Bellamy returned to the house for it while I examined the compass.
William says the cotton is yielding well, though the hired wagon has been costly.
Victoria has nearly finished packing. She intends to leave in a few days.
The evening was clear. I remained in the study until after ten, comparing the figures from the old survey with Grandfather's notes.

Wednesday, October 3, 1888
Clear.
Made a trial of the galvanometer. The first reading was unsatisfactory, but Bellamy discovered a loose terminal and corrected it.
The compass showed no movement beyond what might ordinarily be expected.
William has been occupied with the cotton and complains that the men are not working quickly enough.
Mrs. Hester says the kitchen chimney must be cleaned before the weather turns cold.
I read through the entries concerning October 11 once more. Grandfather wrote very little about what he expected to observe, but he was particular about the hour.

Thursday, October 4, 1888
Cloudy.
The men finished another section of cotton. William believes we may have the greater part gathered before the middle of the month.
Bellamy and I returned to the crossing to test the battery. The current was sufficient.
I have marked the places from which the compass and the galvanometer are to be observed. There must be no confusion when the time comes.
Victoria asked whether I would come to Fort Worth for a few days. I told her I could not leave the property at present.
She said I had been neglecting her. I fear she is right.

Friday, October 5, 1888
Warm.
William was much annoyed with two of the field hands and says the work would go better if Rusk had agreed to take charge of them.
I spent most of the morning at the crossing. Bellamy has become more careful with the instruments, and I have little cause for complaint.
Victoria spoke again of my absence from the house. I told her that the work would soon be finished, though she did not seem reassured.
Paid several small bills and arranged for the wagon to be repaired next week.
Mrs. Hester served roast beef. William remained for supper and talked of little but the harvest.

Saturday, October 6, 1888
Fair.
Victoria departed for Fort Worth this morning. I accompanied her as far as the place where she was to take the next stage of her journey.
She expects to remain with her mother about a fortnight. I promised to write.
Returned to find William arguing with one of the men over the amount of cotton brought in. The matter was settled before dinner.
Went to the crossing in the afternoon. The apparatus appears in good order.
The house is quiet without Victoria.

Sunday, October 7, 1888
Clear and cool.
Did not attend services. Spent the morning reading and the afternoon walking over the property.
William says we shall need several more days of favorable weather to finish the cotton in the western field.
I went to Grandfather's grave and remained there for some time.
His instructions concerning the Book have occupied my thoughts increasingly. I have followed them as closely as I know how, though there are matters in which his notes are incomplete.
Returned before sunset.

Monday, October 8, 1888
Warm.
Bellamy and I checked the electrical connections and made several trial observations.
The compass was steady. The galvanometer responded as expected when the circuit was opened and closed.
William took a load of cotton to the gin. He expects to return tomorrow with the accounts.
Received a short letter from Victoria. Her mother is somewhat improved.
I spent the evening preparing the table of observations. There is no reason the work should not proceed satisfactorily on Thursday.

Tuesday, October 9, 1888
Cloudy.
The men continued gathering cotton. William returned with the gin receipts and was pleased with the weight.
Bellamy and I inspected the shelter at the crossing. It has kept the battery dry.
I examined the old measurements again. There remains a discrepancy between the creek distances and the bearing of the road. It may be no more than an error in the original survey.
The anniversary is now near enough that I find it difficult to attend to other matters.
Slept poorly.

Wednesday, October 10, 1888
Clear.
Went to the crossing early. The compass showed a variation of approximately 11 minutes. The battery and galvanometer were tested and found ready.
Bellamy has been instructed to keep the times exactly and to record every movement of the needle.
Rusk passed along the road in the afternoon. He said Bell was still disputing our right to use the crossing. I told him I would not discuss the matter further until a surveyor had examined the line.
William expects to finish the greater part of the remaining cotton next week.
I reviewed the observations once more after supper.
Everything is prepared.

Thursday, October 11, 1888
Clear throughout the day.
Went to Mercy Crossing with Bellamy before sunset. The apparatus was in good order, and the battery gave a steady current.
The compass readings were as follows:
6:15 — 11 minutes.
6:30 — 10 minutes.
6:45 — 12 minutes.
7:00 — 11 minutes.
7:15 — 14 minutes.
7:30 — 19 minutes.
At 7:45 the variation exceeded one degree. The needle moved slowly and would not hold its position. Bellamy examined the connections and reported no visible defect. The galvanometer also gave an irregular indication.
I stepped away from the instrument to examine the ground near the eastern side of the foundation. Some of the earth appeared loose, though I could not determine whether it had been recently disturbed.
Looking upward, I observed that the stars were no longer visible. The sky was clear, but there remained only one bright light above the eastern timber.
I watched it for what seemed several minutes. The other stars afterward became visible again.
Bellamy was occupied with the battery. By the time he came to where I stood, the appearance had passed. I did not attempt to explain it to him.
The compass gradually returned toward its ordinary reading.
We examined the apparatus once more and found no damage sufficient to account for the disturbance.
Returned to the house late. I have copied the observations as carefully as I can, though I cannot account for what I saw.

Friday, October 12, 1888
Clear and warm.
Returned to the crossing with Bellamy.
The instruments behaved ordinarily. The compass gave a mean variation of approximately 11 minutes, and the galvanometer showed no irregularity.
On examining the equipment, we found that a small case containing fittings was missing. Two lengths of wire and several brass pieces were also gone.
I cannot say when they were removed. Bellamy believes the case was present yesterday, though he is not certain.
There is no proof that anyone has stolen the articles. Nevertheless, I cannot dismiss the possibility that someone has been interfering with the apparatus.
Bell's objection to our use of the road has been much discussed, and I cannot help considering whether he knows anything of the missing property. Yet he supplied much of the equipment himself and has dealt fairly with me. I have no grounds for accusing him.
I asked William to make inquiries, but expressly told him that I wished no confrontation.
Rusk may know who has lately been using the road.
I slept little. Whenever I closed my eyes I saw the single light above the trees.

Saturday, October 13, 1888
Cloudy in the morning. Fair later.
William went out with Rusk to ask about the missing fittings. Several others accompanied them, though I had not asked him to assemble a party.
I reminded him that we had no proof Bell possessed anything belonging to us. William said he understood and that he meant only to ask questions.
Bellamy returned to his lodging near Tolliver Springs this afternoon. He has been much occupied with the instruments and wished to attend to his own affairs.
I spent the greater part of the day examining the observations of the 11th. There is no satisfactory explanation for the movement of the compass. I have considered whether the battery might have affected it, but the arrangement of the apparatus makes this unlikely.
I did not return to the crossing.
William had not told me the result of his inquiries by the time I retired.
Dreamed again of the light, though I cannot remember the circumstances.

Sunday, October 14, 1888
Cool.
News came this morning that Bell's house and adjoining store burned last night.
William and Rusk were among those who went to the property. William says there was an argument, but insists that no one intended violence. I have not obtained a clear account of what occurred.
I am told that Henry Bell and his wife Rebecca have not been found. No one seems to know when they were last seen or whether they departed before the fire.
There is talk of an inquiry. I fear William has involved himself in a matter which may bring considerable trouble upon the family.
Bellamy came during the morning. I told him there had been trouble at New Providence. He asked little, and I did not discuss the Bells' whereabouts.
I have put the observations away.
I remember too well what Grandfather told me of the scandal in Massachusetts and the damage done to the family name. I will not have our affairs made the subject of similar talk.
Slept badly.

Monday, October 15, 1888
Rain before daylight. Cooler.
William went into town this morning. He says there has been much talk of the fire, though no one appears to know what became of Bell and his wife.
I told him to keep away from the matter. He says he has done nothing wrong and will not be made to hide in his own house.
The north wagon needs another wheel. I have instructed that it be taken to the smith before the next load is ready.
Mrs. Hester complains that the flour is nearly gone. Sent a boy into town for another sack and some coffee.
I want the instruments brought home. There is no sense leaving so much valuable equipment beside the creek, where anyone may examine it. Told Bellamy to have the wagon ready tomorrow evening. We shall wait until the road is quiet.
Slept little last night. I keep waking with the feeling that someone is standing near the bed.

Tuesday, October 16, 1888
Clear and pleasant.
Spent most of the day with William over the cotton accounts. The last loads brought less than he expected. He blames the condition of the roads and the charges at the gin.
Rusk came by in the afternoon. He and William spoke outside for some time. I did not join them.
After supper Bellamy and I took the wagon to the crossing. We carried no lantern until we reached the lower road. The instrument was where we left it, covered and dry. We took it down carefully and packed the smaller fittings in a sack.
The stone base remains. I see no reason to disturb it.
We reached the house before midnight and put everything in the locked storeroom.
Bellamy asked whether I meant to return to the crossing. I told him I had not decided.
I was glad to have the instruments home.

Wednesday, October 17, 1888
A fine day.
Had the lower pasture examined. Two sections of fence are down, and the cattle have been getting into the timber.
One of the tenants came to settle his account. There was some disagreement over the weight of cotton delivered, but William says the figures are correct.
I went through the observations of the 11th once more.
The readings before half past seven are clear enough. After that I cannot make much of them. I have written down everything I remember, but I do not trust my memory of the interval.
I have been thinking of Grandfather and his removal from Massachusetts. He must have been younger than I am now when he first learned what had happened to his father. I wonder whether he ever considered another place before choosing Texas.
I slept badly. Dreamed of a road through country I did not recognize.

Thursday, October 18, 1888
Warm again.
The men finished repairing the pasture fence. Paid for nails and two new posts.
Mrs. Hester prepared chicken for supper. It was very good. She says the garden will need clearing before the first hard frost.
Received a letter from Victoria. She is enjoying her visit and expects to return early next week. Her mother has been poorly but is improving.
I wrote back that the harvest is nearly finished and that I shall be glad to have her home.
I have given more thought to our having a child. It has been too easy to put the matter off while I attended to Grandfather's work. I am thirty-three years old and have no son. If anything were to happen to me, the Book would pass into hands that could make nothing of it.
Victoria has wanted children for some time. I have told her that we shall begin trying when she returns.
I said nothing of the fire.

Friday, October 19, 1888
Cloudy. Little wind.
Went into town with William to settle several accounts. Bought lamp oil, writing paper, and a new pair of gloves.
There was talk of the Bells at the store. Some say they have gone west. Others believe they have taken shelter with relations. No one claims to have seen them.
I heard that inquiries are being made concerning the men who went to the property on Saturday.
William says people are making too much of it.
I did not argue with him.
The road home was muddy in places. One of the horses stumbled near the crossing, but no harm was done.
Lay awake until nearly morning. I keep thinking that someone will come to the house asking about my work.

Saturday, October 20, 1888
Rain in the morning.
The last of the cotton in the south field has been gathered. William is satisfied enough with the yield, though he says we should have done better had the weather held.
The men have begun clearing brush along the western fence. There is timber to be cut before winter.
Spent part of the afternoon examining an old map of the western territories. New Mexico interests me. There is land there beyond the settled country, though I know little of the conditions.
William came into the study while I had the map spread across the desk. He asked whether I meant to buy land out west. I told him I was only looking at the country.
He laughed and said I would not last a month away from my books and the comforts of the house.
Perhaps he is right.
I folded the map and put it away after he left.
The instruments remain locked in the storeroom. Bellamy has been paid for his work, and I have told him there will be no further observations for the present.
I am tired, but sleep comes only for an hour or two at a time.

Sunday, October 21, 1888
Clear and cool.
Did not attend services. Read the newspaper after breakfast and spent the afternoon walking about the property.
The fields are nearly bare now. The men will soon turn to repairing fences, cutting timber, and preparing the ground for next year.
William has been in better spirits since the harvest was finished. He speaks of buying another mule and having the north wagon rebuilt. I told him we must first see what remains after the accounts are settled.
Victoria is expected tomorrow. Mrs. Hester has been cleaning the house and has asked for fresh provisions. I sent word for a roast and some vegetables to be brought in.
I shall be glad to have my wife home. The house has been too quiet without her.
I have thought a great deal about our son. Grandfather showed me the names in the Book when I was young, going back farther than any family Bible I have seen. In every generation the first child was a son, and the Book passed to him when his father died. Grandfather said it had always been so, and that it would be so with me. Father died before his own father, which is why the Book came into my hands from Grandfather instead. I know Victoria will bear me a son, just as surely as I know my own name. I must see that he is properly instructed, for Grandfather said there were things in the Book that a man ought not to discover without guidance.
I have not opened the Family Book in several days. It is locked away with the instruments. I believe both are safe for now.
The dreams are less distinct than before, though I still wake in the night.
There is much to be done before winter.
